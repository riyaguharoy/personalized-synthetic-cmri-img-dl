Summary

In 60 seconds

  • What. A generator for synthetic cardiac MRI (a mask and the image) that is steered by a patient's clinical profile, not only by the heart's shape.
  • How. Two steps. A conditional VAE turns 14 clinical features into a heart mask, then an image model (SPADE GAN baseline, then flow matching) turns the mask into a scan.
  • So far. It works end to end. Flow matching beats the GAN (FID 118.8 vs 189.5), and the clinical profile clearly steers the masks (volume correlation 0.95 with it, 0.45 without). A newer mask generator (flow matching, with a filter for failed masks) gives images as good as real masks (FID 116 to 117 against 120), and the 26 features were tested by removing groups (all are kept).
  • Not yet. The VAE masks are still too regular and a flow-matching alternative has too many failed masks, the images from the newest masks are not better yet, and the privacy and usefulness tests are still to do.

Longer backgroundwhy this project, and the full summary

Deep learning for cardiac MRI needs a lot of labelled data, but scans are scarce, differ between scanners and are protected by privacy rules. Generative models can create extra synthetic scans. Most existing work controls only the anatomy of the generated heart, for example with a segmentation mask. This project asks whether the generation can instead be controlled by information about the patient, so that synthetic data can be shaped towards the patient groups a dataset is missing.

The approach is a two-stage pipeline. In stage 1, 14 clinical features (height, weight, chamber volumes, ejection fractions and volume ratios) and random noise are turned into a synthetic segmentation mask by a conditional VAE. In stage 2, that mask and noise are turned into a synthetic cardiac MR image, and two model families are compared: a SPADE GAN (the baseline) and a flow matching model. The results are checked for image fidelity and for whether the clinical features really steer the output. Utility for downstream models and privacy tests follow. The first results are on ACDC, with 85 training and 15 validation patients, and the test sets are kept untouched until the end.

Deep learning models for cardiac MRI need a lot of labelled data, and that data is hard to get. Scans differ between scanners and hospitals, expert labelling is slow, and privacy rules limit sharing. Some patient groups and conditions also appear only a handful of times.

Generative models can create extra synthetic scans. Most existing work controls the anatomy of the generated heart, for example with a segmentation mask. This project asks what happens when the generation is controlled by information about the patient, such as diagnosis group, body measurements or other clinical details, so the synthetic data can be shaped towards the groups a dataset is missing.

Scope. This is a methods and data project. It does not involve clinicians, patient outcomes or clinical deployment, and it makes no claim about clinical impact.

What did the proposal ask?

Can a patient's clinical profile give fine-grained control over synthetic cardiac MR images and their masks, and is the result faithful, useful and private? The proposal splits this into three objectives (build, check, compare) with seven sub-questions.

What is the short answer so far?

Build: yes, a two-stage pipeline works end to end (14 features and noise, then a mask, then an image). Check: the profile does steer the masks (matched features beat shuffled ones), but ejection fraction is only weakly controlled and some masks fail. Compare: flow matching is clearly better than the GAN for the image stage (FID 189 to 119). The comparison with a baseline without clinical conditioning, utility and privacy are still to do.

How the results are judged

  • Did the conditioning work? Measure the generated masks and images and compare with the values requested.
  • Fidelity. How close the synthetic images are to real ones (for example FID, KID, SSIM, LPIPS).

The 14 clinical features

The condition for the whole pipeline: how each feature is calculated from a mask, and why it describes the diagnosis.

Status, 10 Nov. The results on this page use these 14 features. Twelve more candidates (wall thickness, septal thickness and LV/RV circularity, at ED and ES) were chosen from a list of 57 literature features and tested by removing groups. Removing the circularity features did not change the masks or images but lowered the diagnosis read-back (78% against 89%), so all 26 features are kept. Septal thickness and wall-thickness SD help their own control (see the progress log).
Equations and worked examplesheight, weight, volumes, ejection fractions, ratios

The mask model is controlled by 14 numbers that describe the heart. They are the features used to classify the five ACDC diagnoses in the challenge paper by Wolterink et al., which is why they carry the diagnosis information. Twelve of the 14 are calculated from a segmentation mask. Only height and weight come from the patient file. This section explains each one from the beginning.

Step 1: from a mask to a volume

A mask labels every pixel as background, right ventricle (RV), myocardium (MYO) or left ventricle (LV). The heart is scanned as a stack of 2D slices, so a volume is the number of pixels with a given label, added up over all slices, times the volume of one voxel (one pixel times the slice thickness).

Vkph = Nkph × Δx × Δy × Δz ÷ 1000

  • k is the structure: LV, RV or MYO. ph is the phase: ED (end-diastole, the heart is full) or ES (end-systole, the heart has squeezed).
  • Nkph is the number of pixels labelled k, summed over all slices of that phase.
  • Δx and Δy are the pixel size, 1.5 mm after preprocessing. Δz is the slice thickness in mm (it differs between patients, for example 10 mm).
  • Dividing by 1000 converts mm³ to mL.

Worked example (round numbers, not a real patient). If 1,200 pixels carry the LV label over all ED slices, and the slice thickness is 10 mm, one voxel is 1.5 × 1.5 × 10 = 22.5 mm³. The LV volume is 1,200 × 22.5 = 27,000 mm³ = 27 mL.

Worked example for the ejection fraction. If the LV volume is 150 mL at ED and 60 mL at ES, then EF = 100 × (150 − 60) ÷ 150 = 60%. A weak, dilated heart might go from 285 mL to 233 mL, which gives EF = 100 × 52 ÷ 285 = 18%.

Why these features describe the diagnosis

  • DCM (dilated): a very large LV volume and a low EF.
  • HCM (thick muscle): a small LV, a high EF and a high MYO/LV ratio.
  • MINF (after infarction): a reduced EF with a moderately enlarged LV.
  • NOR (normal): values in the middle.
  • RV (abnormal right ventricle): a high RV/LV ratio.

The group means in the ACDC audit and in First results show exactly this pattern. The features were checked against the earlier audit values: the correlation is 0.999 or higher and the mean difference is under 1 mL.

Limits to keep in mind

  • The volumes use the pixel size after resampling to 1.5 mm and the slice thickness from the scan header. Gaps between slices are not modelled.
  • Features 3 to 14 come from the masks, so they describe the heart's size and function, not its texture or the scanner.
  • Wall thickness, shape and radiomics features, which some other papers use, are not among these 14.

Feature set: Wolterink et al., Automatic Segmentation and Disease Classification Using Cardiac Cine MR Images (arXiv 1708.01141), as used in the ACDC challenge (Bernard et al., 2018).

FINDING 1

Fidelity

Flow matching lowers FID from 189.5 to 118.8 and KID from 0.122 to 0.040 with real masks. It is about 20 FID points above the real-against-real floor, against about 90 for the GAN.

FINDING 2

Conditioning

Swapping the features between patients lowers the agreement for every structure, so the profile steers the masks. LV volume and the two ratios follow the request well. Ejection fraction is only weakly controlled (correlation 0.26).

FINDING 3

Cost of generated masks

With the GAN, generated masks cost about 11 FID points compared with real masks. With flow matching the difference is negligible (one set of generated masks).

FINDING 4

Complete two-step generator

Run end to end from the 14 features and noise, with no real mask or image, it scores FID 119.8 ± 2.6 and KID 0.038, the same as flow matching with real masks. The same profile with different noise gives different hearts. Failed masks still pass their errors on to the image.

FINDING 5

The profile matters

A mask model without the clinical profile gives masks that barely follow the real slices (LV r 0.08) and lowers image quality (FID 124.3 vs 119.8). With the profile: LV r 0.64, patient LV volume r 0.95. Ejection fraction stays weak in both.

FINDING 6

Broken masks can be filtered

Only 54% of conditioned masks pass the anatomy checks. Redrawing failing masks lifts that to 100% and keeps the profile effect.

FINDING 7

Better masks, but too regular, and a flow alternative

Findings 4 to 6 use the first mask model (14 features). A newer VAE (feature network with FiLM, Dice loss, 26 features) passes the anatomy checks in about 87% to 92% of the masks without any filter (real masks 93%), against about 40% before. But its masks vary too little: the slice-area spread is 67 to 89% of the real one, apex slices are not small enough, and a size sweep hardly changes the mask. A flow-matching mask generator fixes this (spread about 1.1 times the real one, apex slices shrink, more varied masks) but only 76 to 78% of its masks pass the checks and its RV and myocardium are too large. Both follow the fed features and not the patient’s own (r 0.78 against −0.15). Images from the newer masks scored worse (FID 129 to 133 against 119 with real masks). Details and figures

21 Oct 2026Baseline methods: input masks, image generation, outputsBaseline development
  • Input masks. Each input is one 2D short-axis slice of a labelled ACDC patient, at end-diastole or end-systole. The mask has four labels (background, right ventricle, myocardium, left ventricle) and is given to the model as four one-hot channels at 128×128. Slices with an empty mask are left out. Training uses 1,312 slices from 70 patients, validation 174 slices from 10 patients, and testing 355 slices from 20 patients.
  • How an image is generated. The generator starts from a random noise vector (128 values), turns it into a small 8×8 feature map, and enlarges it in five steps up to 128×128. In every step the mask controls the features through SPADE layers, so the mask decides where the chambers and the wall are, and the model fills in the grey-level texture. The output is a 128×128 grey-scale image.
  • Training. The discriminator sees a mask together with an image and scores patches of it as real or fake. The generator is trained to fool it, and also to stay close to the real image of the same slice (feature matching and an L1 pixel loss). Training takes 150 epochs, about 75 minutes on a free GPU.
  • Output and evaluation. For each mask, a synthetic slice that follows the mask outline. The images are scored with FID and KID against real images from held-out ACDC patients and from M&Ms (see First results). Only generated results are shown on this page, never images from the datasets.
  • Why this model. SPADE is the mask-to-image model used as the baseline in Amirrajab et al. (2022) on M&Ms, and a GAN trains in about 75 minutes on a free GPU. The baseline is meant to be simple and well known, so the effect of clinical-profile conditioning can be measured against it. Diffusion and flow models are options for later.
  • Limits. This is a simplified SPADE: one discriminator scale and no perceptual loss, with an added L1 pixel loss. The L1 loss pulls the output towards one image per mask, so the variety between noise draws is low.
21 Oct 2026Stage 1 model architecture, checked against the codeClinical-profile conditioning
  • Type. A conditional variational autoencoder (VAE): a convolutional encoder and decoder joined by a 16-value latent code, with 2.21 million parameters. There are no skip connections between the encoder and the decoder, so it is not a U-Net.
  • Condition (19 values). The diagnosis group (one-hot), nine measured numbers (LV and RV volume at ED and ES, myocardial mass, LV and RV ejection fraction, BMI, body surface area), slice position, the ED or ES phase, and the three predicted areas. The condition goes into the encoder, and into the decoder together with the latent code.
  • Training (A). The encoder sees the real mask and the condition. The decoder learns to rebuild the mask from the latent code and the same condition. The loss is a class-weighted cross-entropy plus a KL term that keeps the latent code close to a standard normal distribution.
  • Generation (B). No real mask is used. The random forest predicts the areas from the profile, noise is added, the decoder receives a random latent code and the condition, and stray specks are removed by keeping the largest region of each class.

Heart and data termsclick to open
TermMeaning
Cardiac MRI (CMR)A scan that films the beating heart without radiation. The images come from it.

EF (ejection fraction)100 × (EDV − ESV) / EDV, the share of blood pumped out per beat. Roughly 55 to 70% in a healthy LV.

Segmentation maskA label image: every pixel is background, RV, myocardium or LV.
NOR, DCM, HCM, MINF, RVThe five ACDC groups: normal, dilated cardiomyopathy (big LV, low EF), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (thick muscle), previous heart attack, abnormal right ventricle.

Method and evaluation termsclick to open
TermMeaning
ConditioningGiving a model extra information (the clinical profile or a mask) so the output follows it.
VAE / conditional VAEA model that learns to compress and rebuild data through a small random code z. Used in stage 1: features and noise to a synthetic mask.
GANA generator and a discriminator trained against each other. SPADE GAN is the stage 2 baseline.
SPADEA layer that feeds the mask into every block of the generator, so the image keeps the mask's shape.
Diffusion / flow matchingModels that turn noise into an image in many small steps. Flow matching learns the straight direction from noise to image, 50 steps at generation.
Classifier-free guidanceTraining with the mask sometimes dropped (10%), then pushing the output towards the mask at generation (scale 2).
EMAA smoothed copy of the weights during training, used for sampling (decay 0.999).
FIDFréchet Inception Distance (Heusel et al., 2017 [1]). Compares the distribution of real and synthetic images in the feature space of an Inception network: the mean and spread of the two sets are compared, so it measures both quality and variety. Lower is better. Why used: it is the standard measure for generated images, so results can be compared with other papers, and it needs no paired real image. Limits: the Inception network was trained on natural photos, not MRI, and FID is biased when the sample is small [3]. Medical studies find that other feature extractors can rank models differently [4]. For that reason the real-vs-real floor (99.0 here, from two groups of real slices) is always reported, and FID is read together with KID and with the utility and privacy tests.
KIDKernel Inception Distance (Bińkowski et al., 2018 [2]). Uses the same Inception features, but compares them with a kernel distance (MMD) that has an unbiased estimate. Why used: the validation set is small (254 slices), where FID is unreliable, so KID is the second check. Lower is better. It can come out slightly below zero for very close sets.
Matched vs shuffledCheck: compare generated structures with the requested ones, then repeat with the features swapped between patients. A real effect must be lower when shuffled.
UtilityWhether a model trained on synthetic data works on real data (for example segmentation Dice).
PrivacyWhether synthetic images copy training images: nearest-neighbour distance and membership-inference tests.
Patient-level splitAll slices of one patient stay in one set (85 train / 15 validation), so there is no leakage.
Design Science ResearchThe method of the thesis: build an artefact, evaluate it, improve it.
